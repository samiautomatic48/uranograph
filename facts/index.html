Uranium facts

Energy

  • 82 TJ per kg Fully fissioning one kilogram of U-235 releases about 82 terajoules of heat. At about 200 MeV per fission: 22.8 GWh of heat, or roughly 2,800 tonnes of coal equivalent at 29.3 GJ each. world-nuclear.org
  • 450 GJ heat per kg natural U A kilogram of mined uranium yields about 450 GJ of heat in today's reactors. WNA reference cycle (4.5% fuel, 0.22% tails, 45 GWd/t): about 41,500 kWh of electricity, some 19,000 times coal at 24 MJ/kg. world-nuclear.org
  • 360,000 kWh electricity per kg fuel One kilogram of enriched reactor fuel generates about 360,000 kWh of electricity. At 45 GWd/t burn-up and one-third efficiency. A lithium-ion battery stores roughly 0.1 to 0.24 kWh per kilogram per charge. world-nuclear.org
  • 100,000 x fossil fuels (heat per kg) Reactor fuel holds roughly 100,000 times more energy per kilogram than coal or oil. Megajoules per kg: Li-ion battery 0.4-0.9, coal 24-35, oil 41.9, natural gas 53.6, reactor fuel 3.9 million, fully fissioned U-235 82 million. world-nuclear.org
  • 1 pellet One fingertip-sized uranium fuel pellet holds the heat of roughly a tonne of coal. A 7 g pellet at 45 GWd/t releases 24 GJ, matching 1 t of 24 MJ/kg coal; a 5 g pellet, about 0.7 t. world-nuclear.org
  • 5 pellets About five to seven fuel pellets cover an average US home's electricity for a year. A 7 g pellet yields about 2,200 kWh at 45 GWd/t and one-third efficiency; EIA's average home bought 10,791 kWh in 2022. eia.gov
  • 50 million x One uranium fission releases about 50 million times the energy of burning one carbon atom. Roughly 200 million electron-volts per U-235 fission against about 4 electron-volts per carbon atom oxidised in coal, oil or gas. world-nuclear.org
  • 1.05 kg per year A 1 GWe reactor converts about one kilogram of mass into energy each year. 26.2 TWh of heat divided by c squared. Roughly 1.15 tonnes of heavy atoms fission to release it, about 3 kg a day. world-nuclear.org

Fuel cycle

  • 249 t U3O8 per GWe-year One reactor-year: 249 t of yellowcake becomes 27.6 t of fuel and 8.76 TWh. 1 GWe light-water reactor at full output: 4.5% enrichment, 0.22% tails, 45 GWd/t. At a 90% load factor, about 190 tU. world-nuclear.org
  • 180,000 SWU Enriching one reactor-year of fuel takes about 180,000 separative work units. 24.3 tU to 4.5% at 0.22% tails is 7.3 SWU per kg by the standard formula; WNA quotes 182,000. A 5%, 65 GWd/t cycle: about 140,000. world-nuclear.org
  • 88 % of mined uranium Nearly 90% of mined uranium ends up as depleted tails, never entering a reactor. Of 211 tU fed to enrichment for one reactor-year, 24.3 tU becomes 4.5% fuel; 187 tU remains at 0.22% U-235. world-nuclear.org
  • 8.7 kg natural U per kg fuel Each kilogram of 4.5% fuel needs 8.7 kg of natural uranium and 7.3 SWU. At 0.22% tails. For 5% fuel: 9.4 kg and 8.9 SWU at 0.20% tails, or 10.3 kg and 7.9 SWU at 0.25%. world-nuclear.org
  • 3 x annual reload A new reactor's first core needs about three years' worth of fuel at once. The reference 1 GWe core holds 73 tU against 24.3 tU reloaded yearly; WNA puts first loads at 300-450 t natural uranium. world-nuclear.org
  • 2.6 lb U3O8 per kg U 1 kg of uranium equals 2.6 lb of U3O8; 1 lb U3O8 holds 0.385 kg U. U3O8 is 84.8% uranium by mass. One tonne of uranium is 2,599.8 lb U3O8; a million pounds U3O8 is 384.6 tU. world-nuclear.org
  • 0.55 % of mined uranium Only about half a percent of mined uranium is actually fissioned in today's reactors. About 1.15 t fissions per reactor-year out of 211 tU mined. Within the fuel, 45 GWd/t burns under 5% of heavy atoms. world-nuclear.org
  • 0.1 % of reactor output Enriching a reactor's fuel uses about 0.1% of the electricity that fuel will generate. About 182,000 SWU at roughly 50 kWh each in centrifuges is 9 GWh against 8.76 TWh. Old diffusion plants needed 2,500 kWh per SWU. world-nuclear.org
  • 11,400 tU over 60 years A 1 GWe reactor uses about 11,400 tonnes of natural uranium over 60 years. 190 tU a year at a 90% load factor: about 30 million lb U3O8 over 60 years, nearly 40 million over 80. world-nuclear.org

The element

  • 99.27 % U-238 Natural uranium is 99.27% U-238, 0.72% U-235 and 0.005% U-234. Atom fractions. Half-lives: U-238 about 4.47 billion years, U-235 704 million, U-234 245,500. By weight, U-235 is 0.711%. en.wikipedia.org
  • 0.71 wt% U-235 in natural uranium From 0.7% U-235 in the ground to 5% in reactors and over 90% in weapons. LEU is under 20% (power reactors 3-5%); LEU+ 5-10%; HALEU 5-20%; HEU 20% and up; weapons-grade over 90%. world-nuclear.org
  • 19.05 g/cm3 A one-litre carton of uranium metal would weigh 19 kilograms. Density 19.05 g/cm3 at 20 C, about 19 times water. Sources quote anything from 18.7 to 19.1. en.wikipedia.org
  • 1.08 m cube side A year's uranium for a 1 GWe reactor would form a 1.1-metre metal cube. 24.3 tonnes of enriched uranium at 19.05 t per cubic metre is 1.28 cubic metres. The 211 tU mined for it: a 2.2-metre cube. world-nuclear.org
  • 15.4 m cube side All the uranium the world's reactors need in a year fits a 15-metre cube. 68,920 tU required in 2025 (WNA); as solid metal at 19.05 t per cubic metre, about 3,600 cubic metres. world-nuclear.org
  • 3.7 % U-235, two billion years ago Two billion years ago natural uranium was about 3.7% U-235, like today's reactor fuel. U-235 decays over six times faster than U-238, so ore was once rich enough for the Oklo natural reactors. en.wikipedia.org

Market

  • 444 operable reactors 444 operable power reactors worldwide total 407 GWe; 81 more are under construction. WNA count on 17 November 2026, across 31 countries. The US has the most (94), then China (65) and France (57). world-nuclear.org
  • 9 % of world electricity (2025) Nuclear generated a record 2,702 TWh in 2025: about 9% of the world's electricity. Ember puts it at 8.9%, down from 16.6% in 2000. That is about a fifth of all low-carbon electricity. world-nuclear.org
  • 179 million lb U3O8 required (2025) Reactors need about 179 million pounds of U3O8 a year; mines produced 157 million. 68,920 tU required in 2025; 60,213 tU mined in 2024, about 90% of that year's needs. Stockpiles and recycling fill the gap. world-nuclear.org
  • 75 % of mined uranium (2024) Three countries mine 75% of the world's uranium; Kazakhstan alone accounts for 39%. 2024: Kazakhstan 23,270 tU, Canada 14,309, Namibia 7,333, of 60,213 tU. In-situ leaching delivered 55% of world output. world-nuclear.org
  • 42 % from five mines (2024) Just five mines produce 42% of the world's uranium; the top ten produce 62%. 2024: McArthur River/Key Lake 13%, Cigar Lake 11%, Husab 7%, Karatau 6%, Inkai 5%. Kazatomprom, Cameco and Orano mined 49%. world-nuclear.org
  • 43 % of world enrichment capacity (2025) Rosatom holds 43% of world enrichment capacity; four suppliers hold over 99%. 2025, million SWU: Rosatom 29.1, Urenco 17.3, CNNC 13.8, Orano 7.5 of 68.1. Conversion comes from just five companies. world-nuclear.org
  • 45 % of front-end fuel cost Uranium is 45% of nuclear fuel cost, and under 10% of a new plant's total cost. WNA, November 2026 prices: fuel costs $3,950 per kg, or 1.1 cents per kWh. Fuel is 15-20% of new-plant costs. world-nuclear.org
  • 4.9 $ per MWh Doubling the uranium price adds only about $5 to a nuclear megawatt-hour. $1,780 of uranium per kg of fuel, spread over 360,000 kWh. A gas plant's cost moves about $6/MWh per 10% fuel-price change. world-nuclear.org
  • 91.1 % (US, 2025) US reactors ran at a 91% capacity factor in 2025; the world fleet averaged 84%. EIA: between 90.8% and 93.4% every year since 2019, against wind 33.8% and solar PV 24.0% in 2025. eia.gov
  • 103 months, median (2024 grid connections) Reactors connected to the grid in 2024 took a median 8.6 years to build. Seven units, from 61 months (Zhangzhou 1) to 204 (Flamanville 3); mean 114. In 2025, Zhangzhou 2 took 62 months. world-nuclear.org

Geology

  • 2.7 ppm of Earth's crust Uranium is about as common as tin: 2.7 parts per million of Earth's crust. Handbook values: tin 2.3 ppm, silver 0.075, gold 0.004. That makes uranium roughly 36 times silver and 700 times gold. en.wikipedia.org
  • 4 billion tonnes (plus) The oceans hold more than four billion tonnes of dissolved uranium. At about 3.3 parts per billion: roughly 700 times identified land resources, but far costlier to extract. pnnl.gov
  • 16.33 % U3O8 at Cigar Lake Canada's Cigar Lake ore averages 16% uranium oxide; Namibia's Rössing ore, 0.03% uranium. About 420 times richer. One reactor-year's 211 tU means 1,500 t of Cigar Lake ore or 640,000 t at Rössing. cameco.com
  • 85 years Identified uranium resources would cover about 85 years of today's reactor demand. 5.86 million tU recoverable below $130 per kg (January 2025) against 68,920 tU a year. Australia holds 29% of it. world-nuclear.org

History

  • 1789 year discovered Uranium was discovered in 1789 and named after the newly found planet Uranus. Martin Klaproth found it in pitchblende eight years after Herschel spotted Uranus. Péligot first isolated the metal in 1841. world-nuclear.org
  • 1896 year Radioactivity was discovered in 1896 with a uranium salt and a photographic plate. Henri Becquerel's find. Hahn and Strassmann split the uranium atom in 1938; Meitner and Frisch explained fission in 1939. en.wikipedia.org
  • 0.5 watts The first reactor, Chicago Pile-1, ran at half a watt on 2 December 1942. Fermi's team stacked 57 layers of graphite and natural uranium about 6 metres high. It later reached 200 watts. en.wikipedia.org
  • 16 natural reactor zones Nature built reactors first: 16 zones at Oklo, Gabon, about two billion years ago. Found in 1972. They pulsed for a few hundred thousand years at under 100 kW, burning about five tonnes of U-235. en.wikipedia.org
  • 20,000 warheads' worth of uranium Uranium from about 20,000 Russian warheads became US reactor fuel between 1993 and 2013. 500 t of bomb-grade uranium was blended down into 14,446 t of fuel: about a third of US enrichment needs for 20 years. world-nuclear.org
  • 14 % uranium in glaze (maximum) The glaze on vintage red Fiesta dinnerware was up to 14% uranium by weight. Made 1936-1943, then with depleted uranium 1959-1973; a plate holds about 4.5 g. Uranium glass, typically 2% uranium, glows green under UV. orau.org
  • 57 years in service Switzerland's Beznau 1 has generated power since 1969: the longest-running reactor in service. Grid-connected 17 July 1969 and due to run until 2033. In the US, 25 reactors now hold licences to operate for 80 years. world-nuclear.org

Waste

  • 90,000 tonnes of US used fuel All US used fuel since the 1950s would cover one football field under 10 yards deep. About 90,000 tonnes (US DOE, 2022), growing by roughly 2,000 tonnes a year, stored at more than 70 sites in 35 states. energy.gov
  • 27.6 tonnes per GWe-year A 1 GWe reactor discharges about 27 tonnes of used fuel a year. About 26 t of it is still uranium, 1 t fission products and 0.3 t transuranics. As solid pellets, 2.5 cubic metres. world-nuclear.org

Safety and climate

  • 5.6 g CO2e per kWh (life cycle) Nuclear's life-cycle emissions are about 6 grams of CO2 per kWh, the lowest UNECE found. UNECE range 5.1-6.4 g. Medians via Our World in Data: wind 11-17, solar PV 16-53, gas 439, coal 970. IPCC's 2014 nuclear median: 12. unece.org
  • 0.03 deaths per TWh Nuclear causes about 0.03 deaths per TWh, roughly 800 times fewer than coal. Our World in Data, accidents plus air pollution, counting Chernobyl and Fukushima: coal 24.6, oil 18.4, gas 2.8, wind 0.04, solar 0.02. ourworldindata.org
  • 50 x less land than coal Per unit of electricity, nuclear needs about 50 times less land than coal. And 18 to 27 times less than ground-mounted solar, on UNECE life-cycle data. A 1,000 MW plant occupies about one square mile. ourworldindata.org
